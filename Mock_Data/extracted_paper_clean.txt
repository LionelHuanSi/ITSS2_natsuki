[2] 1. Introduction

[3] In agricultural production management, insect pests are responsible for approximately 20 % of global crop losses each year (Mateos Fern´andez et al., 2022). Therefore, insect pest control plays a crucial role in agricultural production and exerts a significant influence on agricultural development, food production, and farmers’ income (Nomura and Shigemura, 2019). Detecting insect pests is a highly challenging task in agricultural image processing (Süt˝o, 2021). Traditional insect pest identification methods heavily rely on frontline plant protection workers and agricultural technicians, requiring substantial time and effort. This approach is cumbersome, inefficient, and inadequate for large-scale pest monitoring, early warning, and environmentally friendly prevention and control. With advancements in computer science, the application of intelligent technologies in agricultural ecological informatics has garnered increasing attention (Amarathunga et al., 2021). Intelligent algorithms not only improve operational efficiency (Wu et al., 2019a), but also effectively prevent large-scale pest outbreaks (Patel and Vaghela, 2019), thereby increasing crop yields and providing data-driven solutions for the sustainable development of agricultural ecosystems.

[4] Image detection methods offer high efficiency, low cost, and simplicity in identifying insect pests. These methods enable timely and accurate detection, allowing agricultural practitioners to implement preventive measures and control their spread (G and Ajai, 2021). As a result, image-based detection methods have become a major research focus in insect pest control in recent years (Lu et al., 2020). Several scholars have proposed traditional image identification methods for insect pest detection, including those based on graph theory (Zelazo et al., 2018) and region testing (Gao et al., 2024). However, traditional detection methods are only effective for a small number of samples or limited ranges, and they demonstrate low stability in detection accuracy (Arshad et al., 2020). As the number of samples increases and their types become more complex, detection accuracy sharply decreases. Deep learning offers high precision and adaptability, overcoming the low detection accuracy of traditional methods when identifying complex and diverse samples (Asefpour Vakilian and Massah, 2013). Nanni et al. proposed an automatic classifier that combines the significance method with a convolutional neural network. The test results demonstrated an accuracy of 61.93 % on the IP102 dataset (Nanni et al., 2020). Li et al. developed a Faster R-CNN-based network model to detect whiteflies and thrips using sticky trap images from greenhouse conditions. This model performed effectively in detecting micro-pests in images with varying insect densities and light reflections (Li et al., 2021a). Albattah et al. proposed a drone-based deep learning method for large-scale, multiclass insect pest detection and classification, using key input points to identify various pest types (Albattah et al., 2023). Zhang et al. developed a trapping system using yellow sticky paper and LED light for automatic insect pest image collection. They also proposed an enhanced YOLOv5 model with copy-pasting data augmentation for pest recognition (Zhang et al., 2022a). Lima et al. proposed a pest detection method based on YOLOv8, improving the mean average precision of small target pests through enhancements to the multi-scale feature fusion module (Lima et al., 2024).

[5] However, the convolutional neural network (CNN)-based algorithm lacks a global understanding of features, limiting its ability to effectively handle occlusion. Moreover, insect pest species often share similar appearances with minimal class differences, making accurate classification challenging for the algorithm. With advancements in image processing, Vision Transformer (ViT)-based image recognition algorithms have demonstrated strong advantages in classification tasks (Dosovitskiy et al., 2020). Guo et al. proposed a multi-pest classification method using Swin-Transformer, applying ViT and multi-label classification to address insect category ambiguity (Guo et al., 2024). However, the high computational cost hindered its deployment in equipment. Peng et al. employed a backbone CNN to extract representative features from input images and introduced an attention-based transformer classification head to identify multiple pests. This approach achieved a classification accuracy of 74.89 % on the IP102 dataset during performance evaluation (Peng and Wang, 2022). Building on these studies, some scholars have combined CNN and ViT to leverage their respective advantages for image recognition tasks. The Detection Transformer (DETR) represents an end-to-end object detection framework built upon ViT architecture (Carion et al., 2020). This framework consists of an input layer, a CNNbased backbone network, a transformer-based encoder, decoder, and detection head (Fig. 1). The model analyzes global image features and identifies occluded objects, enhancing classification performance. Additionally, the model eliminates the need for anchor adjustment and non-maximum suppression (NMS) post-processing, enabling efficient parallel prediction. However, DETR models exhibit slow convergence and reduced detection accuracy for multi-scale targets. Building on this, Lv et al. proposed a real-time target detection model (RT-DETR) that optimizes the DETR model’s encoder. RT-DETR realized both the ability of transformer to analyze global features and the performance of realtime target detection (Lv et al., 2023).

[6] Therefore, in this study, based on the RT-DETR network model, we propose the Pest-RTDetr algorithm for multi-species agricultural insect pest detection. To address the challenges of species diversity, unbalanced pest distribution, and the varying scales and dense arrangements of insect pests in images, we introduce improvement strategies for the classification and localization algorithms. The main contributions of this study are summarized as follows:

[7] 1. We provide a real-time multi-species insect detection method using computer vision, demonstrating its potential for agricultural sustainable development. 2. We identify the key challenges in multi-species pest recognition and introduce a visual attention mechanism to enhance the model’s focus on critical pest features for more accurate localization. 3. We propose a feature fusion method to capture and integrate information from different scales, improving mean average precision in detecting dense, multi-scale pests. 4. We introduce a new loss function to accelerate model convergence and improve classification accuracy.

[8] 2. Materials and methods

[9] Pest detection faces significant challenges, including diverse pest categories, subtle inter-class distinctions, and pronounced intra-class variations. Additionally, data collection is notably more challenging compared to plant disease diagnostics and other applications. Current data is primarily collected through three methods: (1) fixed equipment acquisition, which offers stable lighting but limited background diversity; (2) real-field environment acquisition, providing high-quality images without capture devices, closely resembling actual diagnostic scenarios; and (3) internet data, which is efficient but requires rigorous cleaning and verification. Table 1 illustrates the significant advantages of the IP102 dataset, including 102 pest classes, multiple insect life stages (e.g., eggs, larvae, and adults), and diverse field backgrounds with varying target scales. Additionally, its inherent robustness enhances the model’s generalization capability.

[11] 2.1. Datasets

[12] The IP102 dataset contains 75,222 samples of 102 common crop insect pests, with approximately 19,000 marked in the bounding box for target detection. The images were sourced from specialized agricultural and insect science websites, captured under real field conditions. Based on the varying impacts of different insect pests on crops, each pest is assigned a crop superclass (denoted as super-class hereafter).

[13] To explore the applicability of the IP102 dataset for insect pest detection, we performed data visualization analysis and extracted the pest target sizes from all sample images. The target sizes in the dataset are diverse, with Fig. 2 showing the sample distribution across pest categories. The dataset contains many pest species with an imbalanced distribution across categories, exhibiting a natural long-tail distribution. The data analysis reveals that the dataset features multiple pest species, an unbalanced distribution, and multi-scale characteristics, aligning with real-world conditions.

[14] Additionally, the study incorporated the Dangerous Farm Insects dataset from Kaggle, an RGB image dataset comprising 15 insect pest species (Tarun and Jayyn, 2023). Following a rigorous screening process, 1300 high-quality images were selected and designated as the P15 dataset. In the P15 dataset, pests occupy a wide range of sizes in the images, and most insect pests in the images are densely distributed. In the IP102 dataset, pests exhibit diverse forms and rich colors. Notably, the appearance of certain insect pests varies significantly across life cycle stages (e.g., eggs, larvae, pupae, and adults), complicating the classification model’s ability to extract distinguishing features, making it challenging to group them into a single category (Fig. 3).

[15] 2.2. Data preprocessing

[16] Based on the analysis of data samples in Fig. 2, resampling was employed to expand the dataset and balance training samples through repeated random sampling of tail data. The resampling approach balances pest quantities between head and tail categories while preventing overfitting through controlled tail data sampling. Consequently, the sampled tail data quantity approximates half of the head data. To address the long-tail distribution characteristics, we employed Mosaic (Redmon and Farhadi, 2018) and Mixup (Zhang et al., 2018) for dataset augmentation.

[17] The Mosaic method randomly selects four images, which are then flipped, rotated, cropped, scaled, translated, and adjusted for brightness, saturation, and noise. These images are then stitched together in a random arrangement. This process enriches the multi-scale information and background complexity of insect pests in the training images. The Mixup method combines two images using geometric and pixel transformations in a specified proportion to create a new image. The labels of the two images are also mixed in the same proportion to generate a new label. In this study, the Mixup method was applied to enhance data for insect pests with limited samples, improving model robustness. The Mosaic method was used for other pests to enhance the model’s generalization ability. Fig. 4 brings that the first set of training images was processed using the Mosaic method, while the second set was augmented using the Mixup method.

The backbone network The DAS attention mechanism combines contextual information from layer features with the deformable convolution ability, allowing the model to focus more on salient features without additional memory overhead. As in Fig. 6, input features pass through DAS module. DAS first employs depth-separable convolutions to improve efficiency, followed by deformable convolution to locate relevant image regions, and concludes by outputting the result through dot multiplication with the input features. In this study, DAS was integrated into the ResNet-50 blocks, where gated channel attention facilitates information dissemination between blocks, enhancing CNN performance with minimal increase in FLOPs.

[32] 2.3.2. Feature fusion module In this study, a feature fusion module (CF) is designed to better integrate feature information from different scales. The implementation of CF is provided in pseudocode form, as shown in Algorithm 2. The double-branch structure is employed, where the upper and lower branches undergo CBS (Conv(1 × 1) + SiLU+BN) operations respectively to reduce the number of channels and ensure uniformity between branches. The upper branch incorporates DAS and three stacked Bottlenecks (He et al., 2016). This setup forms the primary gradient flow branch, ensuring lightweight processing while capturing richer gradient information, mitigating gradient vanishing, retaining low-level positional details, and accentuating salient features, thereby improving the accuracy of small target detection. Following the element-wise addition of the lower and upper branches, the resulting output is transformed into Q, K, V vector mode through Flatten, defined in Eqs. (1) and (2). The enhanced CF block preserves inter-layer relationships and integrates features across scales, enhancing global information comprehension and improving the accuracy of multi-scale target localization.

[73] 3.3. Comparative experiment

[74] This study aims to demonstrate the advantages of Pest-RTDetr in insect pest detection tasks. Several commonly used object detection models were tested on the IP102 insect pest image dataset in this study. Table 7 compares Pest-RTDetr with several other models—Faster RCNN (Ren et al., 2017), YOLOv5 (Jocher, 2022), YOLOv7 (Wang et al., 2022), YOLOv8 (Jocher et al., 2023), CenterNet (Duan et al., 2019), DETR, Deformable-DETR (Zhu et al., 2020), DINO-DeformableDETR (Zhang et al., 2022b), and RT-DETR—across training duration, parameter count, detection speed, and mAP. Compared to traditional CNN-based detection algorithms (the first seven models in the table), Pest-RTDetr reduces redundant hyperparameters by eliminating the need for a confidence threshold and NMS. It also incorporates the CF module in the encoder to accelerate model convergence. As a result, Pest-RTDetr achieves a 9.27 % and 4.62 % increase in mAP50 and mAP50–95, respectively, with fewer training epochs than the highest-performing model. In contrast to transformer-based models (the last four in the table), the original DETR algorithm has a large computational load, resulting in slower convergence. Compared to DETR, the optimized Deformable-DETR and DINO-DeformableDETR exhibit lower FPS, despite having similar model convergence speeds, making them less suitable for real-time detection tasks. Pest-RTDetr introduces an efficient pest detection framework that achieves a balance between precision and efficiency through multi-dimensional innovations. The dynamic adaptive attention (DAS) mechanism enables real-time inference at 80 FPS with lightweight parameters (41 M) through differentiable gated dynamic feature weight allocation. The multi-scale feature fusion module integrates shallow high-resolution features with deep semantic features, significantly enhancing small target detection in complex backgrounds. The integrated data augmentation strategy improves the model’s adaptability to complex scenes through multi-graph concatenation and label mixing, while the optimized loss function shortens the training cycle by 30 %. Experimental results demonstrate that the model achieves an mAP of 68.4 % on the IP102 dataset, marking a 9.29 % improvement over the benchmark model.

[75] The comparative evaluation on challenging imagery encompassing diverse pest morphologies, complex environmental backgrounds, and variable population densities reveals distinct performance trade-offs among current detection frameworks. As in Fig. 11, CenterNet has relatively low detection accuracy, likely due to its central point prediction principle, which causes repeated localization of the same target, leading to poor accuracy. Faster RCNN achieves higher pest detection accuracy through its two-stage networks and Region Proposal Networks. However, its post-processing method tends to filter out overlapping targets, resulting in missed detections. The model exhibits lower accuracy in detecting dense targets. YOLOv8, an advanced model in the YOLO series, employs a feature pyramid structure that provides good positioning accuracy for pests of various scales. However, its pest classification accuracy is limited due to the lack of global feature correlation analysis. DETR, a transformer-based detection framework, utilizes a global feature receptive field. Compared to traditional CNN-based detection frameworks, it achieves higher classification accuracy. However, this method suffers from poor localization of multi-scale targets, especially small ones, and a slow model convergence rate. As in Table 7, both the convergence speed and multi-scale target detection performance of Deformable-DETR and DINO-DeformableDETR models have significantly improved. However, their complex multi-scale encoding structures limit real-time performance. RT-DETR effectively localizes pests across different scales and achieves fast detection speed. Building on this, Pest-RTDetr integrates the strengths of CNNs and transformers. The model focuses on local details while considering global feature correlations. Compared to other detection frameworks based on convolutional neural networks, Pest-RTDetr’s global feature correlation analysis enhances the classification performance of pest models under

[78] This study compares several advanced pest detection models. [79] 4. Discussion

[80] This paper proposes an advanced end-to-end multi-species pest detection model, Pest-RTDetr. By enhancing the cross-scale feature fusion module, Pest-RTDetr effectively localizes pest targets across different scales and densities. Compared to existing pest classification frameworks (Peng and Wang, 2022), Pest-RTDetr not only accurately identifies pest categories but also localizes pests in real time, even in complex backgrounds, thus facilitating automated pesticide spraying. This paper also introduces the CF feature fusion module based on an attention mechanism, which enhances the model’s focus on key pest features. Compared to other pest detection systems (Lima et al., 2024; Nanni et al., 2022), our model can accurately identify the head and feet of pests, enabling the differentiation of similar-looking pest species. The experimental results confirm that this method performs well in complex agricultural environments, facilitating intelligent agricultural management. Although Pest-RTDetr performs well in most cases, limitations in the dataset were observed. For instance, datasets often lack coverage of emerging invasive species, causing models to struggle with identifying unknown pests in new environments, which limits their applicability in diverse scenarios. Geographic and climatic coverage is insufficient, and data on complex environments, such as multi-crop systems and rainy conditions, are often lacking. Additionally, the scarcity of data for very small pests in complex backgrounds restricts the generalization capability of the trained models. Despite using data augmentation and crossscale fusion network optimization for image preprocessing, these issues may still affect the model’s generalization ability. Specifically, models require more accurate, broader, and diverse pest datasets to improve their integrated learning capabilities. Leveraging advanced generative models to create additional samples and collaborating with ecological experts to annotate more representative and diverse insect datasets could be beneficial. Additionally, in practical applications, the model may encounter domain shift issues, particularly in detecting pests specific to certain crops. To address these challenges, domain generalization and transfer learning may provide potential solutions. (BravoDiaz et al., 2024; Henghui and Linjing, 2024).

[81] Future development can focus on two key directions: 1. Addressing the model’s performance under specific and extreme conditions, exploring more complex strategies, validating its generalization across different crops and environments, and analyzing the impact of hyperparameter selection on overall performance. This will enhance the model’s practicality and robustness while supporting the development of ecosystems where biodiversity conservation and agricultural production coexist. 2. Designing a multi-modal pest detection model and developing an integrated agricultural decision system that combines soil, weather, and other multi-source data. The system will proactively respond to pest outbreaks and provide resilient support for agroecosystems.

[82] 5. Conclusion

[83] Detecting multi-species insect pests with varying scales, dense distribution, and occlusion in real-world scenarios is a challenging task. This study proposes a method that balances both localization and classification, enabling accurate pest species identification and localization. We aim to address the problem of real-time detection of large-scale, multi-species pests and hope that this work will further stimulate interest among researchers. Future research could build on this study to explore a broader dataset, investigate the model’s adaptability across various ecological environments, and enhance its robustness against noise. This technology will improve agricultural efficiency, promote sustainable agriculture, restore ecological balance, and combat climate change, providing crucial support for the coordinated development of global agriculture and ecosystems.
